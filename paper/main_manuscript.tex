\begin{abstract}
We describe CrashGuard, a low-cost device which detects motorcycle crashes and sends an emergency alert, and we evaluate it by simulation. In the device an ESP32 microcontroller reads a six-axis inertial sensor on the handlebar at 100 Hz, and a small finite-state machine decides whether a crash has happened. A crash is confirmed only if four things happen in order: the motorcycle has been ridden, there is an impact or a sudden unloading, a strong rotation follows within a short time, and the motorcycle then lies tilted and still for two seconds. On confirmation the device switches on a light and a buzzer, and the rider has 15 seconds to cancel before a single event is sent to a cloud service. No physical prototype was built. All results come from generated motion data, from a public motorcycle dataset, and from the unchanged firmware run against simulated peripherals.

On 270 freshly generated events, CrashGuard rejected all 210 non-crash events and detected all 30 high-side crashes, but it missed 17 of the 30 low-side crashes, so its overall sensitivity is 71.7\% (F1 = 0.835). A rule which follows the staged structure of the motorcycle fall detector of Boubezoul et al.\ (2013), run on the same events, detected more crashes (86.7\%) but raised 34 false alarms, and McNemar's test shows that the two rules differ significantly ($\chi^2 = 13.40$, $p = 0.00025$). Sweeps over the tilt, rotation and rest-hold thresholds, one parameter at a time, show that the 2 s rest hold is what removes the false alarms, and that a lower rotation gate could recover some of the missed low-side crashes; this second observation still has to be checked on separate data. Fourteen firmware tests found three defects: the rest timer keeps running across missing sensor reads, a failed cloud connection blocks the main loop for 5 s, and an alert which falls due during a Wi-Fi outage is never retried. A simple battery model gives 12.64 hours of operation at a 120 mA, 5 V load. The code, data and scripts are released, so that every experiment in the paper can be repeated.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} IoT; ESP32; MPU6050; Motorcycles; Detection; Simulation; Alerts; Safety.

\section{Introduction}
A rider who falls from a motorcycle on an empty road may remain unnoticed for some time. A device on the motorcycle which recognises the crash and calls for help can shorten this time, but only if it does not raise false alarms so often that the rider switches it off. The problem is therefore one of detection with two kinds of error, and the two kinds do not cost the same. A missed crash can leave an injured rider without help. A false alarm costs little each time, but frequent false alarms make the device useless. In this paper we describe CrashGuard, a handlebar-mounted prototype built around an ESP32 microcontroller and an MPU-6050 inertial sensor, and we evaluate it. We say at the outset that no physical hardware was assembled; the evaluation uses generated motion data, public motorcycle recordings, and the real firmware run against simulated peripherals. In the rest of this section we go through the earlier work which shaped the design, one paper at a time.

Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm} were among the first to treat fall detection for powered two-wheelers (PTWs) as a problem in its own right. Their main observation is that the lean of a motorcycle in ordinary riding, together with shocks from the road surface, makes a single threshold on acceleration unreliable. They detect a fall in stages: the start of the fall, the growth of the lean angle, and a period of rest after the fall. CrashGuard keeps this staged view. It has to run on a cheap handlebar unit, however, so the stages are written as a small finite-state machine, with a ride-qualification step in front and a cancellation window behind. We compare our detector with a rule which follows the structure of their algorithm, and with two simpler rules, on exactly the same six-axis data.

A few years later the same group published recordings of real PTW falls and critical manoeuvres~\cite{boubezoul2019dataset}. Data of this kind are rare, as crashing a motorcycle on purpose is expensive and dangerous. The falls are the positive examples, and the manoeuvres (hard braking, acceleration in a curve, a badly degraded track, and so on) are negative examples which test whether a detector can stay quiet. We use four falls and seven manoeuvres from this set, and our use of them is limited. We check whether the signals cross the acceleration and rotation gates of CrashGuard, that is, we test the scale of our thresholds and not the full decision. All four falls cross both gates. The unit conversion, filtering and choice of time windows are published with the code.

Gelmini et al.~\cite{gelmini2021novel} developed a crash detection algorithm for two-wheeled vehicles and tested it on experimental riding data. What we take from their work is mainly a lesson about coverage. Ordinary riding produces a wide variety of signals, and a detector which has been shown only a few convenient crash examples tells us little about its behaviour on the road. Our simulated data therefore contain braking, sharp turns, potholes, speed breakers, an impact followed by recovery, and a parked motorcycle tipping over, as well as two kinds of crash. Low-side and high-side crashes are kept as separate classes. This turns out to matter, since in the fresh data every high-side crash is detected and all 17 misses are low-side crashes.

In the literature on human falls, Sucerquia et al.~\cite{sisfall} introduced SisFall, a dataset recorded with a device worn at the waist. Its signals come from the human body and not from a handlebar, so we do not use them. The dataset is useful to us for the way it was designed: it contains many everyday activities which are not falls, so a detector evaluated on it has to show that it stays quiet as well as that it fires. We follow the same principle. Seven of our nine scenario classes are negative, and the class counts are given with every result. On the fresh data CrashGuard rejects all 210 negative events, although this of course holds only within our signal model.

Casilari et al.~\cite{survey} compared the public datasets for wearable fall detection and found large differences in sensor position, sampling rate, recorded activities and recording protocol. A sensitivity of, say, 95\% measured on one dataset may say little about another. The practical consequence for a new study is that the data must be described in enough detail for a reader to judge what the numbers mean. We give the channel units, the sampling rate, the random seeds, the noise and bias levels, the clipping limits and the kinematic transformation used by our generator. Events are never split across the development, validation and test subsets, and a second random stream is used to check that the results repeat.

In a later study Casilari et al.~\cite{cnn} trained convolutional networks on several public fall datasets, and showed that the outcome depends strongly on which repository is used. This made us careful about what our own second dataset can show. A new random seed gives new events, but they come from the same generator, the same mounting position and the same model of the motorcycle. We call the result a fresh-seed evaluation and claim no more for it. The public PTW recordings give evidence of a different kind, about the physical scale of the signals, and the two are reported in separate tables. A stronger test, with whole vehicles or riding sessions held out, is left for future work.

Musci et al.~\cite{musci} used recurrent neural networks (LSTMs) for online fall detection on an annotated version of SisFall. Their model reads a sequence of samples rather than single samples, and we agree that the order of events in time carries most of the information. We encode this order in a different way. Instead of a learned model we use explicit timing rules: a correlation window between impact and rotation, a verification timeout, and a hold time for the rest condition. One advantage of explicit rules is that the same C++ code runs in the offline replay and in the firmware, so the two cannot disagree. Another is that, when a fault is injected, the transition at which the behaviour went wrong can be read directly from the log.

Yu et al.~\cite{kfall} published KFall, a large dataset with temporal labels, intended for pre-impact fall detection. A pre-impact detector, for example one which triggers a wearable airbag, must act within a fraction of a second. Our device acts after the event and has a few seconds to spare. The two problems need different timing measures, and it matters where a delay measurement starts and where it stops. We measure the time from the first candidate sample to confirmation separately from the 15 s cancellation window which follows it. On the fresh data the median of the first is 2.33 s. The firmware tests check that the deadline at the end of the window is met to the millisecond.

Rodegast et al.~\cite{rodegast2024} trained machine-learning models for motorcycle collision detection on simulated accidents and simulated normal riding. Their target is a passive safety system, which must fire quickly and must not fire by mistake. Our target is a warning which the rider can cancel, so a slower decision is acceptable, but nuisance alarms remain costly. Like them, we rely on simulation. Our generated data were produced after the detector rules had been fixed, and every rule we compare receives exactly the same events, so the trade-off between missed crashes and false alarms can be read directly from the confusion counts.

Leoni et al.~\cite{ecall2025} proposed an automatic eCall method for motorcycles based on vehicle dynamics. They distinguish high-side crashes, with fast rotation of the vehicle, from low-side crashes, in which the motorcycle slides out more slowly. This distinction is central to our results. A threshold which suits a fast and violent high-side crash may simply not see a slow slide. In our data CrashGuard rejects every non-crash event, but its low-side recall on the fresh set is only 43.3\%, and we regard this as the main weakness of the detector. We report it separately so that the overall accuracy of 93.7\% is not read as uniform protection.

Tian et al.~\cite{tian2026transformer} used a multi-head attention transformer for fall detection with a sensor worn on the shin. They report a test sensitivity of 90.5\% and an overall accuracy of 93.1\%, and they discuss how class imbalance affects numbers of this kind. Their sensor sits on a human leg and ours on a handlebar, so the numbers cannot be compared, and we do not try to compare them. We cite the work as recent evidence of what learned sequence models can do. From it we also take the habit of reporting the class counts, F1 and sensitivity next to accuracy; with 210 negatives and only 60 positives, accuracy alone would flatter our detector.

Finally, Pradhan et al.~\cite{dual2026} combined a dual-stream transformer with Kalman-filter fusion of inertial signals, and report an F1-score of 91.10\% on SmartFallMM in a subject-wise evaluation. Two ideas from this work seem worth adopting later, namely the fusion of complementary inertial signals and the holding out of whole groups (for us, vehicles or riding sessions) during evaluation. At present CrashGuard uses three hand-designed features and fixed rules. Its contribution lies elsewhere, in a light decision sequence whose every state can be observed and tested.

\subsection{Research Gaps and Demonstrated Improvements}

\subsubsection*{Identified Research Gaps}
Reading the work above, we see three gaps which concern a small device of the kind we build.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{Gap \arabic*:}]
\item \textbf{Thresholds on acceleration alone.} A fixed threshold on the acceleration magnitude cannot both ignore hard road shocks (potholes, rumble strips, speed breakers) and detect slow, low-energy slides.
\item \textbf{Overall accuracy hides missed low-side crashes.} When most test events are negative, a high overall accuracy can coexist with poor detection of one type of crash, typically the low-side slide in which the deceleration is gentle.
\item \textbf{The alert chain is rarely tested.} Most studies stop at classifier metrics. They do not test what the device does when the sensor fails, when the rider cancels, when the cloud service does not answer, or when the battery runs down.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Demonstrated Engineering Contributions}
The staged decision is due to Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm}. Our contributions concern its use in a small handlebar device, and its evaluation.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
\item \textbf{Ride qualification.} A leaky-bucket filter of recent activity, so that a parked motorcycle which tips over, or a knock while standing, is not taken for a crash.
\item \textbf{Local warning and cancellation.} A light and a buzzer, followed by a fixed 15 s window in which the rider can cancel, before anything is sent.
\item \textbf{Comparison on identical data.} CrashGuard is compared with a rule following the structure of Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm} and with two simpler rules on the same events, with paired McNemar tests.
\item \textbf{Threshold sweeps.} The tilt, rotation and rest-hold thresholds are varied one at a time over 29 settings, to see which of them controls the missed low-side crashes and which controls the false alarms.
\item \textbf{Firmware fault tests.} The unchanged firmware is run against simulated peripherals in 14 scenarios, which exposed three defects: in the rest timer, in a blocking network call, and in the missing retry after a network outage.
\end{enumerate}
We repeat that no hardware was built or measured. All the findings come from simulation, from models of virtual peripherals, and from public recordings.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}
\item To implement a 100 Hz six-axis state machine which detects crash-like events on a motorcycle handlebar, with a local warning and cancellation by the rider, and to evaluate it.
\item To compare CrashGuard with a rule following Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm} and with two simpler rules on the primary and the fresh-seed event sets, reporting confusion counts, sensitivity, specificity, F1, delay and paired McNemar tests.
\item To find out, by sweeping the rest-tilt, rotation and rest-hold thresholds, how each of them affects low-side recall and false alarms.
\item To run the firmware with controlled virtual peripherals under sensor, calibration, timing and communication faults, and to record the expected and the actual behaviour.
\item To publish the data, plots, circuit and architecture diagrams and power model, together with a plain account of the limitations.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
We now summarise the same sources by topic, saying what each of them does; the introduction said how each of them affects our design.

\textbf{Motorcycle fall and crash detection.} Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm} gave a simple fall detection algorithm for PTWs, in which a fall is recognised in stages from the motion of the vehicle. The same group later released recordings of PTW falls and critical manoeuvres~\cite{boubezoul2019dataset}, which we use for an external check of signal scale. Gelmini et al.~\cite{gelmini2021novel} studied crash detection for two-wheeled vehicles on experimental riding data and stressed how varied ordinary riding is. Rodegast et al.~\cite{rodegast2024} trained classifiers on simulated motorcycle collisions for fast activation of passive safety systems. Leoni et al.~\cite{ecall2025} gave a dynamics-based eCall method, in which fast high-side motion and slower side slips are treated differently.

\textbf{Human fall detection and its datasets.} SisFall~\cite{sisfall} contains waist-worn recordings of falls and of activities of daily living. Casilari et al.~\cite{survey} compared such public datasets and showed how much they differ in sensor position, sampling and protocol, and in a later study~\cite{cnn} they showed that the results of convolutional networks depend on the dataset used. KFall~\cite{kfall} added synchronised temporal labels and benchmark algorithms for pre-impact detection.

\textbf{Learned sequence models.} Musci et al.~\cite{musci} applied LSTM networks to online fall detection on an annotated version of SisFall. Tian et al.~\cite{tian2026transformer} used a multi-head attention transformer for a shin-mounted device and report 90.5\% test sensitivity and 93.1\% accuracy after class balancing and focal-loss training. \subsection*{Summary of Gaps Addressed}
In short, three gaps motivate the design and the experiments.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Choice of threshold.} A high acceleration threshold misses slow, low-energy falls; a low one fires on ordinary road unevenness.
\item \textbf{Scope of the evaluation.} Accuracy is not enough for a safety device. Calibration, cancellation by the rider, alarm deadlines and the behaviour of the network all affect what happens after a real crash.
\item \textbf{Openness.} Without shared detector code, fixed event datasets and fault traces, other people cannot check the results or find the defects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Problem Statement}
We are given a 100 Hz stream of three-axis acceleration and three-axis angular rate from a motorcycle handlebar. The task is to decide whether, during active riding, the stream contains an impact or an unloading, a correlated rotation, and a sustained tilted rest, in this order. When this is confirmed, the device switches on its local outputs and allows 15 seconds for cancellation before it submits a single IoT event. The evaluation must report false alarms, missed falls, detection delay and the behaviour under faults, by experiments which others can repeat.

\subsection{Dataset generation and preprocessing}
The primary simulation has 675 events, each ten seconds long at 100 Hz, so 675,000 six-axis samples in all. There are nine classes of 75 events. Normal riding, potholes, speed breakers, hard braking, sharp turning, a parked tip-over, and an impact followed by recovery are negative classes; low-side and high-side crashes are positive. This gives 150 positive and 525 negative events. The generator is fixed by the seed 20260828. A second seed, 20260911, gives a further 30 events per class, that is 270 fresh events and 270,000 samples.

Each event is built from roll, pitch and yaw profiles, the gravity vector in the body frame, pulses for the manoeuvre or impact, and noise, bias and clipping. Gravity is expressed in the body frame of the sensor as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:gravity}
\widehat{\mathbf g}_b=
\begin{bmatrix}
-\sin\theta\\
\sin\phi\cos\theta\\
\cos\phi\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{3\times1},\qquad
\mathbf g_b=g_0\widehat{\mathbf g}_b,\qquad
\|\widehat{\mathbf g}_b\|_2=1,
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ and $\theta$ are the roll and pitch angles in radians, and the three entries are ordered along the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes of the sensor (the subscript $b$ stands for body frame). The hat marks a unit vector, so $\widehat{\mathbf g}_b$ is dimensionless and its entries can be read directly in units of $g$. Multiplying by standard gravity, $g_0=9.80665$ m/s$^2$, gives the physical vector $\mathbf g_b$ in m/s$^2$; $g_0$ is a constant and not a fitted parameter. For a level sensor, $\phi=\theta=0$, the vector is $[0,0,1]^{\mathsf T}$. A positive roll gives a positive $y$ component and a positive pitch a negative $x$ component. Yaw does not appear, since rotation about the vertical does not change the direction of gravity.

The linear pulses which stand for the road surface, the rider's inputs and the impacts are added to this gravity vector. To produce the gyroscope signals, the generator does not simply differentiate the Euler angles. It converts the angle rates $\dot{\boldsymbol\Theta}=[\dot\phi,\dot\theta,\dot\psi]^{\mathsf T}$ into body-frame angular velocities $\boldsymbol\omega=[\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z]^{\mathsf T}$ with the standard Tait--Bryan $Z$-$Y$-$X$ (yaw--pitch--roll) relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rigid_body}
\begin{bmatrix}\omega_x\\\omega_y\\\omega_z\end{bmatrix}=
\mathbf E(\phi,\theta)\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\\dot\theta\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}=
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & -\sin\theta\\
0 & \cos\phi & \sin\phi\cos\theta\\
0 & -\sin\phi & \cos\phi\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\dot\phi\\\dot\theta\\\dot\psi\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Here $\psi$ is the yaw angle and $\mathbf E(\phi,\theta)$ is the $3\times3$ matrix which depends only on roll and pitch. When the motorcycle rolls and yaws at the same time, as in a high-side crash, the matrix mixes the angle rates across the three gyroscope axes, which is what a real body-fixed gyroscope would see. At zero roll and pitch the matrix is the identity, and the body rates are simply the angle rates.

The time of the event is drawn uniformly from 3.8 to 5.2 s. The acceleration bias has standard deviation 0.018 $g$ and the sample noise 0.022 $g$; for angular rate the corresponding values are 0.8 and 1.5 $\degree$/s. Samples are clipped to the full-scale ranges of the sensor. The final roll angle lies between 48 and 105$\degree$ for low-side crashes and between 70 and 135$\degree$ for high-side crashes. Note that the low-side range deliberately reaches below the 55$\degree$ tilt gate of the detector, so some low-side crashes cannot be detected by design. Impact pulses are between 3.0 and 11.5 $g$ for high-side crashes and between 0.8 and 5.5 $g$ for low-side crashes, before vector addition and clipping.

For the external signal analysis we load seven critical manoeuvres and four falls from the published PTW supplement~\cite{boubezoul2019dataset}. We keep the finite numeric rows, filter the six channels against aliasing and resample them from 1 kHz to 100 Hz (polyphase resampling with a Kaiser window), convert acceleration from m/s$^2$ to $g$ by dividing by 9.80665, and keep angular rate in degrees per second. The four fall windows are centred at 40.132, 34.288, 35.876 and 43.486 s, each extending $\pm1.5$ s. The manoeuvres are used whole, as negative examples. These short windows test individual gates only; they are not a test of the full decision with rest and countdown.

\subsection{Data split and evaluation protocol}
Within each scenario the primary events are split by the order in which they were generated: the first 45 go to development, the next 15 to validation and the last 15 to test. This gives 405, 135 and 135 events, that is 60, 20 and 20\%. An event is never divided between subsets. The file \texttt{results/splits.csv} lists every assignment.

CrashGuard is a fixed set of rules and has no fitted weights. The split is therefore only a retrospective breakdown of a single replay, and we should not read the test subset as a held-out test in the usual sense. The fresh set of 270 events was generated after the detector had been frozen for this study, and no threshold was chosen by looking at it. It is a second random stream from the same family of scenarios. The public PTW recordings are reserved for the gate analysis, and the human-fall datasets are used only as context.

\subsection{Features, calibration, and decision equations}
At start-up the firmware averages 200 stationary samples. If the norm of the averaged acceleration lies outside 0.85--1.15 $g$, or the RMS of the gyroscope exceeds 5 $\degree$/s, calibration is repeated. Otherwise the averaged acceleration becomes the reference gravity vector, and the three gyroscope means become bias offsets. Let $\mathbf a=[a_x,a_y,a_z]^{\mathsf T}$ be the acceleration vector, $\mathbf r=[r_x,r_y,r_z]^{\mathsf T}$ the reference gravity vector, both in units of $g$, and $\boldsymbol\omega=[\omega_x,\omega_y,\omega_z]^{\mathsf T}$ the angular rates in degrees per second. The detector uses three scalar features. The first is the acceleration magnitude
\begin{equation}\label{eq:accel}
A=\|\mathbf a\|_2=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2},
\end{equation}
where $a_x$, $a_y$ and $a_z$ are the components sampled at the same instant and divided by $g_0$. Thus $A=1$ means one $g$, and the thresholds 2.0 and 0.35 used below mean $2g$ and $0.35g$. In the firmware the raw signed counts are divided by 2048 counts per $g$, which is the scale for the $\pm16g$ range, and a new sample arrives every 10 ms.

The second feature is the magnitude of the angular rate,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rotation}
\Omega=\|\boldsymbol\omega\|_2=\sqrt{\omega_x^2+\omega_y^2+\omega_z^2},
\end{equation}
where $\omega_x$, $\omega_y$ and $\omega_z$ are the bias-corrected rates about the three sensor axes, in degrees per second. The raw values are divided by 16.4 counts per degree per second (the $\pm2000\degree$/s range) before the calibration means are subtracted. Taking the norm makes the gate indifferent to the axis along which the dominant rotation happens, and to its sign. The detector compares $\Omega$ with 90$\degree$/s when looking for a rotation and with 30$\degree$/s when checking for rest; these are thresholds and not part of the formula.

The third feature is the tilt, the angle between the present acceleration vector and the reference,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tilt}
\Theta=\frac{180}{\pi}\arccos\!\left[\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf r}{\|\mathbf a\|_2\|\mathbf r\|_2},-1,1\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf r=a_xr_x+a_yr_y+a_zr_z$ is the dot product, $\mathbf r$ is the mean of the 200 accepted calibration samples, and $\operatorname{clip}(u,-1,1)=\min(1,\max(-1,u))$. Dividing by the two norms gives the cosine of the angle, the clipping protects $\arccos$ against round-off just outside its domain, and the factor $180/\pi$ turns radians into degrees, so that $\Theta\in[0,180]$. If the product of the norms is below $10^{-6}$ the implementation returns a tilt of zero. Since $\Theta$ is computed from the instantaneous acceleration, it measures the lean of the vehicle properly only when the motion is small, that is, during the rest after a fall, which is where we use it.

The decision proceeds as follows. Riding activity is present when $|A-1|\geq0.08$ or $\Omega\geq4\degree$/s. Evidence of activity accumulates up to 1000 ms and decays at half that rate when there is no activity, and while the evidence is full a 5000 ms deadline for active riding is refreshed. Once riding is qualified, a candidate starts when $A\geq2.0$ or $A\leq0.35$. A rotation with $\Omega\geq90\degree$/s must occur within 750 ms of the candidate. After that the detector waits for the rest condition
\begin{equation}
 \label{eq:rest} R=(\Theta\geq55\degree)\land(0.70\leq A\leq1.30)\land(\Omega\leq30\degree/\mathrm{s}),
\end{equation}
where $\land$ is logical AND, so $R$ is true only when the motorcycle is strongly tilted, the acceleration is close to $1g$ (within $\pm0.30g$), and the vehicle is hardly rotating. The comparisons include their limits. The predicate is instantaneous; the time requirements are imposed by the state machine, not by Equation~\eqref{eq:rest}.

A crash is confirmed when $R$ has held for 2000 ms, and the verification is abandoned 6000 ms after the candidate. Confirmation starts the alarm and the 15,000 ms cancellation window. The state machine needs constant memory and constant work per sample.

\subsection{Baselines and metrics}\label{sec:baselines}
We compare CrashGuard with three other rules. The \emph{acceleration-only} rule fires as soon as $A\geq2.0$. The \emph{strict ordered} rule needs $A\geq4.0$, then $\Omega\geq200\degree$/s within 500 ms, and then a tilt of at least 60$\degree$ with $0.70\leq A\leq1.30$ held for three seconds, all within eight seconds of the impact. The third rule follows the staged structure of the motorcycle fall detector of Boubezoul et al.~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm}, and we refer to it by their name. It has three stages. A fall starts when the roll rate $|\omega_x|$ reaches 60$\degree$/s, or when $A\geq2.5$ or $A\leq0.40$. The lean must then reach $\Theta\geq55\degree$ within 2 s, otherwise the rule resets. Finally the rest condition $\Theta\geq55\degree$, $0.70\leq A\leq1.30$, $\Omega\leq30\degree$/s must hold continuously for 1.5 s, and the attempt is abandoned 6 s after the start. We should be clear that these thresholds are our own choices, written to follow the structure of the published method; we do not claim that they are the exact values of the original paper, so this rule is an approximation of their detector and not a copy. Unlike CrashGuard, it has no ride qualification.

Let $TP$, $TN$, $FP$ and $FN$ be the numbers of true positives, true negatives, false positives and false negatives. All of them are counts of whole events, and in every experiment below the denominators of the metrics are positive. The first metric is accuracy,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:accuracy} \mathrm{Accuracy}=\frac{TP+TN}{TP+TN+FP+FN},\end{equation}
the fraction of all events which are classified correctly. The denominator is the size $N$ of the evaluation set, $N=675$ for the primary set and $N=270$ for the fresh set. As there are many more negative events than positive ones, accuracy can be high even when many crashes are missed, which is why we never report it alone.

Sensitivity (also called recall, or the true-positive rate) is the fraction of crashes which are detected,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sensitivity} \mathrm{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}.\end{equation}
The denominator is the number of labelled crashes, 150 in the primary set and 60 in the fresh set. This is the metric which shows missed crashes directly, and we also give it separately for low-side and high-side crashes. For example, the fresh CrashGuard result is $43/(43+17)=0.717$, that is 71.7\%.

Specificity is the fraction of non-crash events which are correctly rejected,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:specificity} \mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\end{equation}
with 525 negatives in the primary set and 210 in the fresh set. It describes nuisance alarms, but only within the negative events we chose to generate, so it has to be read together with how many and how varied these events are. For the fresh CrashGuard result $TN=210$ and $FP=0$, so the observed value is 1. This is a finite sample, and below we give a Wilson interval for it rather than claim perfect rejection.

Precision is the fraction of detections which are real crashes,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:precision} \mathrm{Precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.\end{equation}
The denominator counts all events predicted positive, whatever their true class, so precision depends on how common crashes are in the test set. If a rule predicted no positive event at all, the ratio would be undefined and our code would return zero; this never happens in the results reported here. For CrashGuard on the fresh set, precision is $43/(43+0)=1$.

The F1-score combines precision and sensitivity,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f1} F_1=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN},\end{equation}
which is the harmonic mean of the two written in terms of counts; the subscript 1 means that both are weighted equally. F1 is penalised by both kinds of error, and we report it as a decimal between 0 and 1, whereas accuracy, sensitivity and specificity are given in percent. For the fresh CrashGuard counts, $F_1=86/103=0.835$.

The false-positive rate is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fpr} \mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{TN+FP},\end{equation}
the fraction of non-crash events which raise an alarm, which is one minus the specificity. For the acceleration-only rule on the fresh set, $FP=108$ and $TN=102$, so $\mathrm{FPR}=108/210=51.4\%$; for CrashGuard the numerator is zero. Note that this is a rate per event. A rate of false alarms per hour of riding would need the riding time as its denominator, which our data do not provide.

The delay of a detection is the time of confirmation minus the time at which the successful candidate started, and it is computed only for detected crashes. The acceleration-only rule has, by definition, no confirmation hold and thus zero delay. For a proportion $\hat p=x/n$ with $x$ successes out of $n$ events, we give 95\% Wilson intervals~\cite{wilson1927interval},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wilson}
\begin{aligned}
p_{\mathrm L}&=\frac{\hat p+z^2/(2n)-z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{1+z^2/n},\\[2mm]
p_{\mathrm U}&=\frac{\hat p+z^2/(2n)+z\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n+z^2/(4n^2)}}{1+z^2/n},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $p_{\mathrm L}$ and $p_{\mathrm U}$ are the lower and upper limits of the interval, and $z=1.959964$ is the 97.5th percentile of the standard normal distribution. For sensitivity $x=TP$ and $n=TP+FN$, and for specificity $x=TN$ and $n=TN+FP$. The constants 2 and 4 come from the Wilson score construction. Unlike the simple normal interval, the Wilson interval stays informative when all observed events are successes, as happens for our specificity. The intervals describe uncertainty due to the finite number of events; they say nothing about whether the generator resembles real riding.

Finally, to test whether two rules differ on the same events, we use McNemar's test with Edwards' continuity correction~\cite{mcnemar1947},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mcnemar}
\chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c},
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the number of events which CrashGuard classifies correctly and the other rule does not, and $c$ is the number of events for which the opposite holds. Events on which the two rules agree do not enter the statistic. Under the null hypothesis that both rules have the same error rate, $\chi^2$ follows a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom. When there are few discordant events ($b+c<25$) we use instead the exact two-sided binomial $p$-value, $p = 2 \sum_{k=0}^{\min(b,c)} \binom{b+c}{k} 0.5^{b+c}$.

\section{Experimental Results and Discussion}
\subsection{Experimental conditions}
The C++17 programs are compiled with optimisation and strict warnings. Python with NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib generates the data, replays the detector, computes the metrics and draws the plots. Every event is processed by a freshly reset detector. The firmware harness uses a virtual clock, a 14-byte register buffer, instrumented GPIO and tone outputs, and a local Blynk-compatible sink. It runs the unchanged firmware and returns, on request, a failed probe, a truncated read, a lost link or a cloud timeout. No network credentials are needed.

We re-ran the pipeline on the 675 primary events and obtained the same confusion counts as before, and then added the 270 fresh events, so 945 events and 945,000 samples were processed in total. Besides these, we ran 14 firmware scenarios, 39 DC operating points and nine battery-discharge cases. These experiments test different layers of the system, and we report their counts separately.

\subsection{Timing, cancellation, and IoT communication}
The median delay from candidate to confirmation is 2315 ms on the primary set and 2330 ms on the fresh set. The strict ordered rule needs 3120 and 3130 ms, and the Boubezoul rule 2280 and 2325 ms. Most of the delay is therefore the rest hold itself. With the intentional 15 s cancellation window added, the time from the candidate to the event deadline is 17.33 s at the fresh median, before any delay in connecting to the service.

In the harness, riding is qualified first, then a rotation is injected at 1500 ms and an impact at 1600 ms. The tilted rest starts at 1610 ms, confirmation follows at 3610 ms, and the event falls due at 18,610 ms. The test checks that the state is still \texttt{COUNTDOWN} at 18,600 ms and becomes \texttt{ALERT\_DUE} ten milliseconds later. After confirmation GPIO26 is high and the tone is 2400 Hz. The simulated service receives exactly one \texttt{crash\_confirmed} event, whose description is 143 bytes long.

In a separate test the cancel button on GPIO27 is pressed at 4010 ms. The outputs go off, and the detector stays idle until the end of the run at 20,000 ms. The event path distinguishes four outcomes: submission to the service, a disabled configuration, a Wi-Fi failure and a cloud failure. The event description gives the acceleration, angular rate and tilt at confirmation, and states that no location fix is available, since the device has no GNSS receiver. The event identity and payload follow the Blynk firmware event interface~\cite{blynkevents}.

The virtual-hardware image together with the state traces works as a replay dashboard, in which the sensor values, the countdown, the alarm outputs and the event outcome all belong to one run. A deployed application could show the same fields through a Blynk device template. Continuous telemetry on virtual pins would be an extension; in the sketch we evaluate, the only remote operation is the event submission.

\subsection{Threshold sensitivity and calibration analysis}
To see how much the results depend on the chosen thresholds, we varied one parameter at a time, kept the others at their configured values, and replayed the 675 primary events for each setting. The parameters and ranges were the following.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The rest tilt threshold $\Theta_{\mathrm{tilt}}$, 13 values from 40$\degree$ to 70$\degree$ in steps of 2.5$\degree$.
\item The rotation gate $\Omega_{\mathrm{rot}}$, 10 values from 60 to 150$\degree$/s in steps of 10$\degree$/s.
\item The rest hold $T_{\mathrm{hold}}$, 6 values from 1.0 to 3.5 s in steps of 0.5 s.
\end{enumerate}
This gives 29 settings in all. Note that the sweep uses the same events on which the configuration was developed, so it describes how the rules behave and is not an independent test. The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\safeincludegraphics[width=\linewidth]{threshold_sensitivity.pdf}
\caption{One-at-a-time threshold sweeps on the 675 primary events: (a) rest tilt threshold, (b) rotation gate, (c) rest hold. Each panel shows overall sensitivity, low-side recall and specificity; the dotted line marks the configured value (55$\degree$, 90$\degree$/s, 2.0 s).}\label{fig:sensitivity}

\textbf{Tilt.} Specificity stays at 100\% for every tilt threshold down to 50$\degree$. From 47.5$\degree$ downwards two non-crash events are accepted (99.6\%). Within the range without false alarms, the tilt threshold matters surprisingly little: sensitivity is 76.7\% at 50--52.5$\degree$ and 76.0\% from 55$\degree$ to 62.5$\degree$, a difference of one event. Going down to 45$\degree$ recovers four more low-side crashes (low-side recall 57.3\%, overall 78.7\%) at the price of the two false alarms. Above 62.5$\degree$ sensitivity falls, and at 67.5$\degree$ and 70$\degree$ even high-side crashes begin to be lost (98.7\% and 96.0\%); at 70$\degree$ overall sensitivity is 70.7\%. We conclude that the tilt gate is not the main cause of the low-side misses.

\textbf{Rotation.} Specificity stays at 100\% over the whole range from 60 to 150$\degree$/s, while low-side recall depends strongly on the gate. It is 62.7\% at 60 and 70$\degree$/s, 52.0\% at the configured 90$\degree$/s and only 10.7\% at 150$\degree$/s. Thus on these data a rotation gate of 70$\degree$/s would detect eight more low-side crashes (overall sensitivity 81.3\% instead of 76.0\%) without a single false alarm. This is perhaps the most useful outcome of the sweep, and it shows that the configured point is not the best point without false alarms. We have not changed the configuration, however, because the observation comes from the development data and the rejection of negatives may rest largely on the other conditions; a lower rotation gate needs to be checked on held-out events before it is adopted.

\textbf{Rest hold.} Here the picture is reversed. Sensitivity hardly moves (76.7\% at 1.0 and 1.5 s, 76.0\% from 2.0 s), but specificity drops to 97.7\% (12 false alarms) at 1.5 s and to 96.0\% (21 false alarms) at 1.0 s. The sweep does not record which classes these false alarms come from. A likely source is the impact-with-recovery class, whose generated lean lasts between 0.7 and 2.1 s, but we have not verified this. Longer holds bring no gain in specificity, and since the hold is part of the confirmation delay, every extra half second of hold adds about half a second of delay by construction. So the 2 s hold is the shortest hold, among those tried, which removes all false alarms.

All fourteen assertions pass. For T9, T11 and T13 this means that the known defect is reproduced. We describe the three defects in turn.

\textbf{T9: rest-timer continuity.} The rest timer measures elapsed time between timestamps and does not check that samples actually arrived in between. If the I\textsuperscript{2}C reads fail during the rest, the time still counts. In the test a valid rest sample at 1610 ms is followed by bus failures at 2000 and 3000 ms and by a valid sample at 3610 ms, and the crash is confirmed although the sensor was silent for 2000 ms. The fix is to restart the rest timer whenever the gap between valid samples exceeds, say, 50 ms.

\textbf{T11: blocking connection.} The cloud connection call is synchronous and has a 5000 ms timeout. When the service cannot be reached, the call stops the main loop for those 5 s and sampling deadlines are missed. Production firmware needs a non-blocking network state machine.

\textbf{T13: no retry.} If an incident falls due while Wi-Fi is down, it is skipped and the state moves to \texttt{ALERT\_COMPLETED}. When Wi-Fi comes back, nothing is sent. The event has to be stored and retried.

The GPIO and tone assertions concern only the commanded outputs. A disconnected piezo or a dead LED cannot be detected by the present firmware, which has no feedback from its actuators. Checking that the outputs physically work would need additional sensing, and we leave it for future work.

\subsection{Power simulation}\label{sec:power}
The battery model is a reservoir of usable charge which is drawn down step by step; the operating time is an output of the model, not an input. The initial charge is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:charge_initial}
q_0=C_{\mathrm{nom}}f_{\mathrm{use}}=(2.6\,\mathrm{Ah})(0.90)=2.34\,\mathrm{Ah},
\end{equation}
where $C_{\mathrm{nom}}=2.6$ Ah is the nominal capacity of the cell, $f_{\mathrm{use}}=0.90$ is the fraction of it which we treat as usable, and the subscript 0 marks the start of the discharge. Both inputs are fixed parameters of the simulation.

During discharge the cell voltage falls linearly with the remaining charge,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cell_voltage}
V(q)=V_{\min}+(V_{\max}-V_{\min})\frac{q}{q_0}
     =3.0\,\mathrm V+(1.2\,\mathrm V)\frac{q}{q_0},
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the remaining usable charge in Ah, with $0\leq q\leq q_0$, so that $q/q_0$ is the state of charge. The voltage is $V_{\min}=3.0$ V when the cell is empty and $V_{\max}=4.2$ V when it is full. The model is deliberately simple; a real lithium-ion cell has a flatter curve in the middle.

The boost converter holds the output at a fixed voltage, so the current drawn from the cell grows as the cell voltage falls. At integration step $k$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cell_current}
I_{\mathrm{cell},k}=\frac{V_{\mathrm{out}}I_L}{\eta V(q_k)},
\end{equation}
where $q_k$ is the remaining charge, $V_{\mathrm{out}}=5.0$ V is the output voltage, $I_L$ is the load current (90, 120 or 180 mA) and $\eta$ is the converter efficiency (0.80, 0.90 or 0.95). The numerator is the output power in watts, and dividing it by the efficiency and the cell voltage gives the cell current in amperes. All nine combinations of load and efficiency were simulated.

The charge is then updated by a forward Euler step,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:charge_update}
q_{k+1}=q_k-\frac{I_{\mathrm{cell},k}\Delta t_s}{\kappa_t},\qquad
\Delta t_s=1\,\mathrm{s},\quad \kappa_t=3600\,\mathrm{s\,h^{-1}},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_s$ is the time step and $\kappa_t$ converts ampere-seconds to ampere-hours. The iteration stops after the first step at which the remaining charge is no longer positive, so the last step may overshoot zero by less than one step's worth of charge.

For the nominal case, $I_L=0.120$ A and $\eta=0.90$, the cell is empty after 45,490 steps of one second, that is after 12.64 h. Over the nine cases the runtime ranges from 7.49 to 17.78 h. Figure~\ref{fig:energy} shows these runtimes; each point is one complete integration and each curve keeps the efficiency fixed while the load varies.

\fig{energy.pdf}{.85}{Simulated runtime for the nine combinations of 5 V load (90, 120, 180 mA) and boost efficiency (80, 90, 95\%). The model includes the falling cell voltage and the converter loss.\label{fig:energy}}

As a check on the numerical model we can integrate it in closed form. For the same linear voltage profile and constant load and efficiency, the runtime in hours is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runtime}
t_{\mathrm{lin}}=\frac{\eta q_0(V_{\min}+V_{\max})}{2V_{\mathrm{out}}I_L},
\end{equation}
where all symbols are as above, and $(V_{\min}+V_{\max})/2=3.6$ V is the mean cell voltage. The units reduce from Ah$\cdot$V/(V$\cdot$A) to hours. For the nominal case the formula gives 12.636 h, which agrees with the numerical result to within the one-second step.

For comparison, if the cell voltage is held constant at $V_{\mathrm{const}}=3.7$ V, with the other values unchanged, the runtime $t_{\mathrm{const}}=\eta q_0V_{\mathrm{const}}/(V_{\mathrm{out}}I_L)$ is 12.99 h. The difference of 0.35 h comes only from using 3.7 V in place of the mean of 3.6 V. Neither calculation includes ageing of the cell, temperature, or the voltage sag under short bursts of load such as Wi-Fi transmission.

Part~A shows the trade-off which runs through this paper. CrashGuard gives up sensitivity on low-energy falls (71.7\% against 86.7\% for the Boubezoul rule) in return for no false alarms at all (specificity 100.0\% against 83.8\%). It seems plausible that riders would switch off a device which often raises false alarms, and in that case the trade is worth making; but we have no data on rider behaviour, and the question should be settled by a field study rather than by argument.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
The main weakness of the detector is its low-side recall. CrashGuard missed 17 of the 30 fresh low-side crashes (43.3\% recall), and these account for all its missed crashes in both sets. In a low-side crash the motorcycle slides down without a large impact, and the fixed gates do not see the slower ones. The sweep in Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity} suggests where to start: a lower rotation gate recovered eight low-side crashes in the development data without false alarms. This has to be confirmed on held-out events. Beyond that, one could track the lean angle adaptively, use the vehicle speed from wheel sensors or GNSS, or train a small sequence model for slow slides.

The generator uses proper rigid-body kinematics (Equation~\eqref{eq:rigid_body}), but much of the physics of a real motorcycle is missing from it: the travel of the front suspension, the flexibility of the frame, the nonlinear friction between tyre and road, engine vibration, and resonance of the mounting bracket. The detector must eventually be tested on instrumented crash tests and on long road recordings, over different surfaces and mounting positions. Note also that the low-side generator itself places some final roll angles below the 55$\degree$ gate, so part of the low-side miss rate is a property of our data and part a property of the detector, and we have not separated the two.

The comparison with Boubezoul et al.\ depends on our own choice of thresholds for their staged method (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). A different and equally reasonable choice could move their sensitivity and specificity, and so the conclusions of Table~\ref{tab:fresh} about that rule should be read with this in mind.

The firmware tests revealed three defects which must be fixed.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Rest-timer continuity (T9).} The rest timer must restart whenever the gap between valid samples exceeds a freshness bound, say 50 ms.
\item \textit{Blocking network call (T11).} The synchronous 5000 ms connection call must be replaced by a non-blocking network state machine, so that sampling is not delayed.
\item \textit{Event persistence (T13).} An incident which falls due during a network outage must be stored in non-volatile memory and retried, with back-off and deduplication, when the link returns.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, the device cannot tell the emergency services where the rider is, and it has never been built. A low-power GNSS receiver, or a Bluetooth link to the rider's phone, would supply the location. A real board, tested on a vibration bench and sealed against water and dust (IP67, say), is needed before anything can be said about the device outside simulation.

\section{Conclusion}
We have described CrashGuard, a crash detection and emergency alert prototype for powered two-wheelers, and evaluated it by simulation. A 100 Hz six-axis state machine decides whether a crash has occurred; a light and a buzzer warn the rider, who then has 15 seconds to cancel before an event is sent to the cloud.

On 270 fresh simulated events CrashGuard detected all 30 high-side crashes and rejected all 210 non-crash events, but it missed 17 of the 30 low-side crashes. Its overall sensitivity is therefore 71.7\%, with 100.0\% specificity, 93.7\% accuracy and F1 = 0.835. On the 675 primary events the corresponding values are 76.0\% sensitivity, 100.0\% specificity and F1 = 0.864.

A rule following the staged method of Boubezoul et al.\ (2013) detected more crashes on the same fresh events (86.7\%) but raised 34 false alarms (83.8\% specificity; F1 0.712), and McNemar's test shows that the two rules make significantly different errors ($\chi^2=13.40$, $p=0.00025$). The one-at-a-time sweeps show that the 2 s rest hold is what removes the false alarms, that the tilt threshold has little effect within the range without false alarms, and that a rotation gate of 70$\degree$/s instead of 90$\degree$/s would detect eight more low-side crashes on the development data without false alarms. This last point is the obvious next experiment.

Running the unchanged firmware against simulated peripherals proved useful in its own right: it found three defects before any hardware existed, namely the rest timer running across missing reads, a blocking cloud connection, and the missing retry after a network outage. The battery model gives 12.64 hours at the nominal 120 mA, 5 V load.

We have tried to report the weaknesses of the system as plainly as its strengths, and to make every number in the paper reproducible from the released code. We hope that this makes CrashGuard a useful starting point for others who build low-cost safety devices for two-wheeler riders.
