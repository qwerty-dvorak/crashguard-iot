\begin{abstract}
CrashGuard is a low-cost prototype for detecting fall-like motorcycle events
and starting a rider-cancellable emergency alert. It combines an ESP32,
handlebar-mounted MPU-6050, LED, piezo alarm, cancel button, and optional Blynk
notification. Physical hardware was unavailable for this review, so the
evidence is limited to deterministic data replay, an independent
powered-two-wheeler dataset audit, native tests, target compilation, and three
Wokwi virtual-hardware scenarios. The CrashGuard state machine correlates
acceleration, rotation, orientation change, and post-event rest instead of
using a single impact threshold. On 675 generated ten-second events it
produced \CrashGuardTP{} true positives, \CrashGuardTN{} true negatives,
\CrashGuardFP{} false positives, and \CrashGuardFN{} false negatives,
corresponding to \CrashGuardSensitivity{} sensitivity and
\CrashGuardSpecificity{} specificity. These are
simulation results and must not be interpreted as road-crash accuracy. The
study also shows that the original 4 g and 200 $\degree$/s rule is too strict
for the closest public motorcycle data. The outcome is a testable prototype
and a clear plan for later bench and road validation.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Evidence statement.}
No physical crash, battery-runtime test, or network-delivery measurement is
claimed. Wokwi verifies virtual sensor, firmware, button, and GPIO behavior;
it does not reproduce vehicle mechanics or prove field safety.

\section{Introduction and scope}

India recorded 172,890 road deaths in 2023, including 77,539 two-wheeler
users~\cite{morth2023}. CrashGuard addresses only the delay between a
fall-like inertial event and an alert attempt. It does not prevent crashes,
estimate injury, guarantee Internet access, or replace emergency services.

The project investigates three questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:}]
  \item Can impact, rotation, and tilted rest reject high-dynamic non-crash
        riding events better than acceleration alone?
  \item Is a strict 4 g and 200 $\degree$/s ordered rule consistent with independent
        powered-two-wheeler recordings?
  \item Can one compact decision method run in native replay and on an ESP32
        while producing the expected virtual-hardware behavior?
\end{enumerate}

The main contribution is an interpretable state machine with a complete
electrical design, repeatable simulated event set, public-data sanity check,
and automated Wokwi scenarios. Machine learning is deferred because no large,
representative, labelled dataset exists for this exact vehicle and mounting
position.

\section{Evidence from the base paper and related work}

The supplied pIOT base paper uses a shin-mounted LSM6DSM, 50 Hz windows, and a
CNN-transformer for human fall recognition~\cite{tian2026transformer}.
CrashGuard adopts its useful principles---six-axis temporal context, separate
sensitivity and specificity, and hardware-aware design---but not its model or
score. Table~\ref{tab:basecompare} makes the difference explicit.

The paper was treated as evidence to audit, not final truth. Its stated ROC
point $(\mathrm{FPR},\mathrm{TPR})=(0.055,0.905)$ implies an AUC of at least
$0.905(1-0.055)=0.855$ for any valid nondecreasing ROC curve, which conflicts
with the reported AUC of 0.768. It also describes the fixed-point format as
both 8-bit and Q7.8, and the downloaded archive does not contain a complete
manifest for reproducing the paper's final windows, labels, augmentation, and
subject splits. Therefore, neither its numerical result nor model is reused
as CrashGuard performance evidence.

The base-paper percentages must not be compared numerically with CrashGuard's:
the sensing body, label definition, dataset, split, and evaluation unit all
differ, and CrashGuard has no physical target-domain test set.

Motorcycle-specific research provides the stronger design basis. Boubezoul
et al. published a rule-based powered-two-wheeler detector and released four
fall plus seven extreme-manoeuvre recordings~\cite{boubezoul2013algorithm,
boubezoul2019dataset}. Gelmini et al. designed and experimentally validated
two-wheeler crash logic~\cite{gelmini2019validation,gelmini2021novel}.
Mahariba et al. applied conventional machine learning to the Boubezoul data
~\cite{mahariba2022ml}, while Lee et al. compared threshold, supervised, and
unsupervised e-scooter methods~\cite{lee2024escooter}. These studies support
multi-feature temporal reasoning, but their results are not directly
comparable because placements, vehicles, labels, and test protocols differ.

\section{System design}

The MPU-6050 is sampled at 100 Hz. Detection remains local on the ESP32. A
confirmed event activates the LED and piezo, then starts a 15 s cancellation
window. If the rider does not cancel, the firmware makes a bounded SSL
connection through a phone hotspot and calls Blynk IoT's
\texttt{Blynk.logEvent} with event code \texttt{crash\_confirmed}
~\cite{blynkevents}. The event must be created in the Blynk template with
timeline and notification delivery enabled. The hotspot supplies connectivity only;
it does not give the ESP32 the phone's location. No coordinates are therefore
invented.

The MPU-6050 uses $\pm16g$ and $\pm2000\degree$/s ranges to reduce immediate
saturation during rapid motion~\cite{mpu6050datasheet}. GPIO34 is input-only
and belongs to ADC1, making it suitable for battery sensing while Wi-Fi is
active~\cite{esp32datasheet}. A 100 $\mu$F rail capacitor and local 100 nF
capacitors reduce short supply disturbances. The selected TP4056 module is a
charger with protection, not a power-path controller; the prototype must be
switched off while charging.

At an estimated average 5 V load of 120 mA, the rail power is 0.60 W. With a
3.7 V cell and assumed 90\% boost efficiency,
\begin{equation}
 I_{cell}\approx\frac{0.60}{3.7(0.90)}=0.180\ \mathrm{A}.
\end{equation}
Using 90\% of a 2600 mAh cell gives an ideal estimate of about 13 h. This is a
sizing calculation, not a measured runtime.

\section{Data and decision method}

Three data sources have different roles, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:data}.
The HealthLINK archive was downloaded to audit the base-paper provenance but
was excluded from motorcycle metrics because its labels and sensing body do
not match this problem~\cite{healthlinkarchive}. The Boubezoul powered-
two-wheeler recordings provide an independent feature check. The generated
set provides repeatable complete-event replay.

The generated set uses seed \SimulationSeed{} and nine groups of 75 events:
normal riding, pothole, speed breaker, hard brake, sharp turn, parked
tip-over, impact with recovery, low-side crash, and high-side crash. Only the
last two are positive. Attitude trajectories produce body-frame gravity;
linear pulses model road and impact effects; angular rates come from attitude
derivatives. Bias and sample noise are added before clipping to the selected
sensor ranges. This improves realism over arbitrary threshold crossings, but
it remains a model developed alongside the detector.

For acceleration $\mathbf a_k$ and angular rate $\boldsymbol\omega_k$ at
sample $k$, the main features are
\begin{equation}
 A_k=\|\mathbf a_k\|,\qquad
 \Omega_k=\|\boldsymbol\omega_k\|,
\end{equation}
and tilt relative to the stationary startup gravity reference $\mathbf r$ is
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_k=\cos^{-1}\!\left(
 \operatorname{clip}\frac{\mathbf a_k\cdot\mathbf r}
 {\|\mathbf a_k\|\|\mathbf r\|},-1,1\right).
\end{equation}

CrashGuard first requires recent ride-like activity. It then opens a
candidate when acceleration is at least 2.0 g or at most 0.35 g. Rotation of
at least 90 $\degree$/s may occur up to 750 ms before or after this event.
The candidate is confirmed only if tilt reaches 55$\degree$ while acceleration
remains between 0.70 and 1.30 g and angular rate stays below 30 $\degree$/s
continuously for 2 s. Failure to establish rest within 6 s rejects the event.
These rules are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:parameters}.

The comparison uses: (1) acceleration at or above 2 g alone, (2) a strict
ordered rule requiring 4 g, then 200 $\degree$/s, then three seconds of rest,
and (3) CrashGuard. The first two are comparison methods, not older releases
of CrashGuard. No method is trained or fitted on the event labels.

\section{Implementation and Wokwi validation}

The production firmware directly configures and reads the MPU-6050, samples
at 100 Hz, calibrates bias and upright gravity, applies the state machine,
handles the active-low cancel button, and drives the alarms. The same portable
C++ decision logic is used for native dataset replay, avoiding disagreement
between the reported algorithm and deployed code. Native tests cover rotation
before impact, pothole rejection, parked tip rejection, and cancellation.

Compilation with ESP32 Arduino core 3.3.11 and Blynk library 1.3.5 succeeds,
using 1,040,343 bytes of flash (79\%) and 49,124 bytes of static RAM (14\%).
The firmware starts Wi-Fi without blocking detection and connects to Blynk
only when the cancellation deadline expires. A submitted event is distinguished
from an unavailable network and from verified phone receipt. Build size shows
feasibility only; timing jitter, energy use, and long-duration stability still
require physical measurement.

The Wokwi circuit contains an ESP32-DevKitC virtual board, MPU-6050, LED,
piezo, button, and battery-sense potentiometer. It uses the same GPIO numbers
as the physical design. UART is connected explicitly so the
test runner can observe firmware events. Wokwi sensor controls apply values in
g and degree/s~\cite{wokwidiagram,wokwimpu,wokwiscenarios}.

The virtual crash, pothole, and cancellation scenarios were executed once and
passed with Wokwi CLI 0.26.1. The checked-in offline build contains no Blynk
credentials and explicitly reports that the event was skipped. A separate
opt-in scenario expects \texttt{BLYNK\_EVENT\_SUBMITTED} when a dedicated Blynk
test device is configured over Wokwi's \texttt{Wokwi-GUEST} network; it is not
reported as a completed phone-delivery test.

\section{Results and interpretation}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} and Figure~\ref{fig:performance} report event-level
results on 150 positive and 525 negative generated events. The
acceleration-only baseline is sensitive but produces 289 false positives. The
strict ordered rule avoids false positives but misses 111 of 150 simulated
crashes. CrashGuard recovers 75 of those misses without adding a false positive
in this set.

The CrashGuard sensitivity interval is [\CrashGuardSensitivityLow{},
\CrashGuardSensitivityHigh{}]. Its observed specificity is 100\%, but the lower
Wilson bound is \CrashGuardSpecificityLow{}; zero false positives in 525 generated
events does not prove that false alarms are impossible. Median confirmation
delay is \CrashGuardConfirmationDelayMs{} ms, which reflects the required two-
second stable-rest interval. All 75 high-side events were detected, while 36
of 75 lower-dynamic low-side events were missed. This is the main simulated
failure mode.

The independent PTW audit supports lowering the original thresholds. None of
the four published fall intervals reached 4 g or 200 $\degree$/s, whereas all
four passed the CrashGuard high-or-low acceleration gate and all four passed its
90 $\degree$/s rotation gate. Several non-fall manoeuvres also cross an
individual CrashGuard gate, confirming why temporal correlation and final-rest
evidence are necessary. The external archive does not provide a reliable
gravity reference for reproducing the full state machine, so it is not
reported as a complete CrashGuard accuracy test.

\section{Limitations and safety}

The following limitations define the valid scope of the results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The generated signals omit frame flex, mounting slip, engine
        harmonics, varied impact locations, weather, sensor drift, and rider-
        vehicle separation.
  \item The external PTW set contains only four controlled falls, while the
        human dataset has the wrong sensing domain.
  \item The generator and thresholds were developed in the same project; no
        blinded physical holdout has been evaluated.
  \item Event specificity is not a false-alert rate per hour or kilometre.
  \item Wokwi does not validate the cell, charger, boost converter, mounting,
        piezo loudness, Wi-Fi coverage, HTTPS delivery, or Blynk receipt.
  \item The prototype is not a certified automotive, medical, or emergency
        device. Intentional public-road crash testing would be unsafe.
\end{itemize}

Future validation should proceed in stages: bench-check voltages, sensor
orientation, sounder current, sampling jitter, and charge isolation; collect
unmanned stationary tip-overs and suspended-frame drops into soft
containment; then log ordinary riding over potholes, braking, turns, weather,
and mounting variations. Thresholds should be frozen before a held-out route,
and operational results should include false alerts per hour and kilometre.

\section{Conclusion}

CrashGuard combines impact, rotation, tilt, rest, and rider cancellation in a
compact ESP32 state machine. In deterministic simulation it improves on the
strict ordered comparison and avoids the acceleration-only method's high
false-positive count. Public motorcycle recordings also show that the 4 g and
200 $\degree$/s thresholds are not portable. However, 36 simulated low-side
events remain missed, so physical validation must precede stronger claims.
